History & Philosophy of Physics

1. The Meaning and Facets of `Symmetry' in Physics[2610.12488]
Abstract

This paper is an English translation of a contribution to the latest German edition (2017) of Hermann Weyl's classic text "Symmetry", edited and annotated by Erhard Scholz, Klaus Volkert, and myself. In this contribution I discuss various physical aspects of the notion of symmetry and place them in context from the perspective of theoretical physics. In particular, a distinction is drawn between symmetries of individual configurations (solutions of equilibrium equations or equations of motion) and symmetries of the laws themselves. Likewise, a distinction is made between those operations that alter physical states and so-called gauge symmetries, which leave physical states unchanged and merely represent a redundancy in the mathematical description. Besides gauge symmetries, I comment on the connection to conservation laws, symmetry breaking, and the philosophy of space.

2. On the Utility of Textbooks in Understanding the Renaissance of General Relativity[2610.13562]
Abstract

This paper argues for the historical utility of general relativity textbooks written during, or shortly before, the Renaissance of general relativity. More so than research papers, textbooks offer a narrative account of the field–one that may differ substantially from the accounts of other authors addressing the same subject. Textbooks on general relativity therefore constitute a distinctive form of testimony to the intellectual forces that shaped general relativity, particularly during its reinvention period from 1955 to the mid-1970s. A second pillar of this argument is the observation that nearly all principal investigators at the research centers previously identified by Lalli et al.–centers that functioned as intellectual epicenters of the Renaissance of general relativity–eventually produced a textbook associated with their own school of thought. To situate this observation within the broader development of the field, the paper presents a concise history of general relativity textbooks from the theory's inception in 1915-16 to the 1980s. This history illustrates one way in which textbooks can serve as sources for reconstructing the intellectual dynamics of the Renaissance of general relativity.

3. A Review on the Properties and Nature of Light[2610.13932]
Abstract

The following paper is a detailed review on the properties and nature in relation to the phenomena known as 'light'. The paper tries to chronologically detail the development of theories and related experiments from the philosophical Greco-Roman and Indian schools of thought to the modern quantum theories.

4. Determinism and Indeterminism as Model Artefacts: Toward a Model-Invariant Ontology of Physics[2512.22540]
Abstract

This paper argues that, where deterministic and indeterministic formulations agree on empirically corroborated structure while differing in their completion of structure that remains empirically underdetermined, features invariant across these formulations warrant ontological commitment, whereas any warrant attaching to features that vary across them is categorically more provisional. Because deterministic and indeterministic completions are always formally available, while additional grounds for preferring particular completions are contingent and defeasible, empirically corroborated structure alone cannot determine whether reality is fundamentally deterministic or indeterministic, or whether that very distinction belongs only to specific representations of it. On this basis, I advance a model-invariance criterion according to which structural features are candidates for realist commitment insofar as they are empirically corroborated within a regime and preserved across the admissible formulations of that structure. I show how such invariant content can be characterised in mathematically intelligible ways, yielding a fallibilist form of structural realism grounded in model-invariance rather than in the specific form of any given mathematical representation. Features such as conservation relations, invariant measures, symmetries, and probability or correlation structures over observable histories may satisfy this criterion. By contrast, the localisation of modal selection—whether in fine-grained initial conditions, stochastic outcomes, or state-update and collapse mechanisms—may vary across empirically admissible formulations and therefore constitute a gauge-like representational freedom. This framework offers a realist ontology for modern physics that combines empirical openness with resistance to metaphysical overreach.

Physics Education

5. Measuring the Speed of Light with a Time-of-Flight Setup: An Authentic Experiment for High School[2610.12614]
Abstract

Students often perceive scientific experiments as clean, straightforward procedures with predictable outcomes. Students' beliefs about the nature of experimental physics typically remain inconsistent through the initial stages of undergraduate education. In reality, experimental work in physics involves hands-on activities such as soldering circuits, assembling provisional setups, and building functional mock-ups from simple materials before reliable measurements can be achieved. To address this gap, we designed a laboratory activity in which students were completely involved in the experiment design, construction and operation with the final goal of measuring the speed of light with a low-cost and compact setup. The educational objective of this activity was double: firstly, to make students aware of the importance of experimental choices and constraints in the setup design and secondly, to discover the potentiality of semiconductor technologies. As far back as the 1990s, with the spread of compact disk players, laser diodes and photodiodes capable of fast timing performance in the order of nanoseconds became largely available. In the last decades, consumer electronic devices made high performance technologies ubiquitous. Nevertheless, the idea of measuring ultrashort time intervals and the relevance of such measurements in science and technology remain unknown to most students. Nowadays, digital instruments with significant performance such as waveform generators and oscilloscopes are affordable for high school physics labs, paving the way to a closer contact with the world of experimental physics.
