History & Philosophy of Physics

1. The two-component picture of soft hadron production: Regge exchange, duality, and resonance cascades[2611.14388]
Abstract

The fragmentation regions of soft hadronic collisions approach energy-independent limits. Benecke, Chou, Yang, and Yen stated this hypothesis of limiting fragmentation in 1969. Feynman gave it a parton-model formulation the same year. Measurements from the CERN Intersecting Storage Rings (ISR) to the Large Hadron Collider (LHC) still show this approach. Fischer and co-workers interpret the NA49 spectra from the CERN Super Proton Synchrotron (SPS) as the sum of a target and a projectile component, each the decay of an excited hadronic system. This review traces the theory behind that two-component picture. It examines the hypothesis that the hadronic tools that predate quantum chromodynamics (QCD) suffice for the soft, non-perturbative domain of QCD. Regge exchange organizes the fragmentation region in triple-Regge form. It supplies the diffractive mass law of the excited systems, with the shrinkage of the Pomeron in its transfer dependence. Finite-energy sum rules and the two-component duality of Harari and Freund equate averaged towers of resonances with that exchange. Duality therefore motivates describing the excited systems in hadronic degrees of freedom. The review follows the cascade description through its predecessors, namely the fireballs of Niu, the novas of Jacob and Slansky, the clusters of Hwa, Good-Walker diffraction, and the two-stage dissociation of Hama. HEART (Hadronic Excitations And Resonance Transitions) assembles these pieces into an event-level cascade of two-body hadronic transitions. A companion paper ( arXiv:2611.11570 ) defines it and tests it against the NA49 data. The review closes with the fragmentation-region measurements that would separate a resonance cascade from a string, namely the isospin of forward baryons, the local balance of strangeness in associated production, and forward resonance signals with their mass tails.

2. Deflating the Spacetime-Matter Dichotomy[2512.10775]
Abstract

In this paper we analyse scalar-tensor theories-specific instances of which include mainstream inflation and dark energy models-in light of the spacetime-matter dichotomy. We argue that it is difficult to categorise the scalar fields as either a pure aspect of the spacetime structure or a pure form of matter, by focusing on the Jordan vs Einstein frames of these theories. We present and evaluate various interpretational options available, concluding that the spacetime-matter dichotomy becomes untenable in this context. At the same time, the ontological and conceptual category of spacetime can be decoupled from that of gravity, with the latter remaining viable in the context of scalar-tensor theories.

3. Heat as gauge[2503.08753]
Abstract

We show that thermodynamics can be viewed as a gauge theory. In particular, it is a principal R-bundle over a space of observable work configurations, with gauge transformations representing changes in the 'hidden' contributions to energy not captured by work. Heat defines a connection, and its curvature measures deviation from local equilibrium. Summed negative curvature on a disc is shown to give an expression of the Clausius inequality on the oriented loop bounding that disc. This allows one to derive a geometric expression of the Second Law. The framework further allows one to exhibit a thermal analogue of the Aharonov-Bohm effect. Instead of producing geometric phase shifts, this effect produces geometric entropy shifts.

4. Invariant universal averages and Bertrand's paradox: a response to Shackel[2611.00981]
Abstract

In 2014, with D. Aerts we proposed a solution to the hard problem of Bertrand's paradox by means of a universal average over different ways of randomizing an entity. In his recent monograph, N. Shackel raises objections concerning the approximation by cellular densities, the restrictions used in their construction, the apparently trivial limit of their averages, the choice of a second-order probability law, and the dependence of the result on the naming of chords. We revisit these objections using the invariance-based formulation of the universal average. The first objection is well taken, and the approximation argument is repaired: an explicit sequence of bivalent cellular densities provides weak approximation of any prescribed probability measure, with an error bound for intervals. We show that any second-order selection law invariant under rearrangements of equal-length cells has the same uniform barycenter, independently of the particular densities and weights employed. For cellular densities, this conclusion holds exactly at every finite resolution. Thus, non-uniqueness of the second-order law does not prevent uniqueness of the average it produces, which is what entitles one to speak of 'the' universal average at all. Finally, we prove that no probability measure can be invariant under both these rearrangements and arbitrary changes of interval lengths. The reference geometry therefore cannot be obtained by averaging alone: it must be supplied by the physical modelization of the randomized entity. These results clarify the scope, and the limitations, of our original distinction between the easy and hard problems of Bertrand's paradox.

5. AI is ready, but are the students. From answer-making to sense-making[2611.13404]
Abstract

Students in introductory physics often struggle with interpreting kinematics graphs, especially when connecting graphical features to physical meaning. We investigated AI tool use in kinematic graphing analysis as an intervention to support active sensemaking. This study examines GPT-5 performance on the Test of Understanding Graphs in Kinematics (TUG-K) and the way students interacted with GPT-5 during introductory physics activities involving graphical analysis. For the first part of the study, GPT-5 responses were evaluated across repeated TUG-K trials and compared to previously reported GPT-4 performance. Following that analysis, student-GPT-5 dialogue episodes were collected and analyzed using an inductive coding scheme. GPT-5 performance results show markedly improved accuracy. Student-GPT-5 dialogue episodes evolved from mostly procedural answer-making requests early in the term, to more reflective sensemaking interactions at the end of the term, suggesting that instructional framing is needed for AI tools to function as meaningful peer-like learning supports.

6. Active learning at a graduate summer school: feasibility, student response, and the role of instructor training[2611.13583]
Abstract

Active learning is becoming standard practice in undergraduate STEM instruction in North America and is slowly being adopted in graduate courses. Graduate summer schools, one- to two-week programs in which students from many institutions gather for intensive, specialized instruction in their field, are common in physics; they are taught by researchers chosen for their expertise, and their pedagogy remains almost entirely traditional lecturing. We report a study at a one-week graduate school in neutrino and astroparticle physics attended by 38 students from 8 countries. Six lecturers with limited or no prior experience of teaching with active learning introduced Peer Instruction-style active learning into their lectures after approximately four hours of preparation led by a physics-education specialist. Each of the three main lecture streams was taught traditionally on one day and in the active format on the other. Students were surveyed after each of the first two days and at the end of the week; all six instructors were surveyed as well. In every active lecture, spanning three instructors and three topics, 82-100% of respondents reported that the interactive segments helped them stay engaged and check their understanding, and in every stream the difficulty was rated as better matched on the active day. At the end of the week, 33 of 36 wanted such teaching in future graduate courses, and not one respondent disagreed. All six instructors judged the approach productive and intend to use it again; only two found implementation straightforward. With brief expert-led preparation, lecturers new to active learning implemented it successfully for a heterogeneous audience, and both students and instructors want more. The school was also the first time any of the six instructors implemented structured active learning: a summer school can develop its lecturers as well as its students.

7. Spacetime contactability in Galactic civilization networks: Contact counts and percolation[2611.12923]
Abstract

The number of Galactic civilizations does not determine whether they are in contact. Allowing civilizations to appear at different times separates listening, audibility, mutual-listening, and relay networks, with a distinct reply-closing condition requiring a return signal within the sender's lifetime. For each network we derive the contact counts and estimate the connectivity threshold in a uniform population, then apply the results to a structured Galactic disc, separating the detection range $d_{\rm max}$ from the light horizon $cL$ through $\chi=d_{\rm max}/cL$. The contactability is $\eta=\pi n d_{\rm max}^{2}$, where $n$ is the active civilization surface density. Averaged over civilization appearances, the lifetime listening count is $2\eta$ for all $\chi$ and, at fixed detection range, for any lifetime distribution. Audibility and mutual-listening counts sum to $4\eta$, but connectivity depends on threshold behavior rather than counts alone. As $\chi\rightarrow0$, the listening threshold decreases from the coeval value $4.51$ to $1.32\pm0.01$. Assuming an ideal complete survey, our own non-detection gives the reference bound $\eta(R_0)\le2.75$ at the Sun's Galactocentric radius. This benchmark lies above the listening, audibility and relay thresholds throughout the range studied. Unlike the coeval model, the spacetime model can satisfy both this benchmark and a local connectivity criterion, moving the local-contact frontier outward by $3.2$ kpc. Under the same benchmark, civilizations hear at most $5.5$ others on average, while the mean reply-closing count falls below unity for $\chi\gtrsim1$. Contact may therefore be common even when completed replies remain rare.
