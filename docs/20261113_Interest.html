1. History, mathematics and physics: two cases of the inverse square law[2611.16700]
Abstract

How did mathematicians of the past relate to history? What can we learn from their experience? It is a truism to say that all people are embedded in history. It impacts on them in ways that they may or may not realise. At the two extremes, some may be completely oblivious; others may deliberately set out to change the historical narrative and the course of history. In this chapter, I explore these questions through two case studies. Both are of eminent British mathematicians/physicists in the late 1800s and their handling of an inverse square law of force. Both case studies show ways in which these men were blind to the history of their subject, conscious of it but limited by it, or deliberately trying to use it. First, I look at William Thomson's and James Clerk Maxwell's attempts to establish the inverse square law of electrostatics in the 1850s to 1870s. I ask the question, why did Maxwell devote the last years of his life to editing the 100-year-old electrical researches of Henry Cavendish and repeating Cavendish's experimental verification of the inverse square law of electrostatic repulsion? Second, I discuss John Henry Poynting and Newton's law of gravitation in the 1880s and 1890s, asking why Poynting preferred to describe his experiments as measurements of the mean density of the Earth rather than of the gravitational constant.

2. Gravitational observables from a post-Carrollian expansion[2611.14644]
Abstract

Einstein gravity, with a few exceptions, only admits nonlocal observables. This feature is often attributed to diffeomorphism invariance, since a gravitational field can thereby locally be transformed away. We show this attribution to be incorrect by constructing infinitely many exact ultralocal Dirac observables in (velocity dominated aka electric) Carroll gravity. The latter describes Einstein gravity's ultrarelativistic limit, is fully diffeomorphism invariant, and of independent interest in a variety of contexts. Starting from these Carroll observables a systematic construction of Dirac observables for Einstein gravity is outlined. The resulting "post-Carrollian" expansion is organized by the number of spatial gradients or fractional inverse powers of Newton's constant, where each partial sum is local.

3. Physical Observables and Superselection[2611.17051]
Abstract

It is well-known that a physical state may be represented by a unit vector in a Hilbert space, whilst a physical observable may be represented by a self-adjoint operator on that same space. However, the introduction of a superselection rule on a Hilbert space imposes constraints such that not all self-adjoint operators can represent observables, which has further consequences for the states themselves as well as the physical processes that transform them. In this article, we review the concept of superselection from the view of how such rules would affect what we consider to be physical. Arguments for the existence of a superselection rule due to charge or possibly energy conservation are given and the emergence of effective superselection rules due to decoherence or the lack of a shared reference frame are also discussed.

4. What Can Actually Be Proved About the Einstein-Podolsky-Rosen Paradox? Operational Consistency, Locality, and Quantum Completeness[2604.13135]
Abstract

The Einstein-Podolsky-Rosen (EPR) argument concerns whether accurate quantum predictions imply a complete description of physical reality. We distinguish predictive adequacy, operational no-signalling, EPR elements of reality, and ontological completeness. We formalize the EPR reality criterion using observable-, value-, and context-dependent predicates and prove that its incompleteness implication does not entail a prohibition on objectively stochastic origins of outcomes. A two-observable stochastic-source countermodel establishes this limited logical non-entailment, without addressing randomness generated at measurement or reproducing the full quantum assemblage. We illustrate the latter distinction using an established two-setting qubit steering inequality. For continuous variables, we derive inferred-variance and Gaussian-steering criteria for a stipulated instantaneous elastic-collision map, studying mass ratios, incident wave-packet spreading, covariance inflation, and readout noise. We distinguish losses of fixed-axis Reid violations from losses of optimized Gaussian steering. These results are conditional benchmarks, not predictions for the proposed finite-duration ion-collision apparatus, whose scattering dynamics and preparation and measurement parameters remain unspecified. We also show why uncertainty inequalities and exponential decay memorylessness alone do not establish intrinsic ontological randomness. The analysis delimits the logical and physical assumptions needed for claims about EPR completeness without proposing a new hidden-variable dynamics.

5. Hands-on fabrication of 2D material field-effect transistors for the classroom using off-the-shelf ionic gel[2611.14565]
Abstract

We introduce a hands-on experimental method that enables students to fabricate and operate van der Waals semiconductor-based field-effect transistors (FETs) using readily available materials and minimal infrastructure. The approach is based on ionic-gel gating using commercially available hydrogel conductors originally developed for muscle electrostimulation devices. For device fabrication we rely on commercially available thin-film interdigitated electrode arrays and for the semiconductor channel, we deposited MoS2 flakes produced by high-throughput mechanical exfoliation. By avoiding the need for cleanroom processing, vacuum deposition, or custom electrolytes, this method allows functional FETs based on van der Waals materials to be realized in a standard teaching laboratory. The experiment is well suited for undergraduate and early graduate courses and is intended to complement concepts introduced in electronics and solid-state physics by providing students with first-hand experience in the fabrication and electrical characterization of emerging electronic devices. Representative electrical measurements demonstrate gate-dependent modulation of the channel conductance and illustrate characteristic features of ionic-gated transistor operation. Overall, the proposed platform provides an accessible route for students to fabricate, characterize, and analyze field-effect devices without access to conventional semiconductor foundry.

6. Active learning at a graduate summer school: feasibility, student response, and the role of instructor training[2611.13583]
Abstract

Active learning is becoming standard practice in undergraduate STEM instruction in North America and is slowly being adopted in graduate courses. Graduate summer schools, one- to two-week programs in which students from many institutions gather for intensive, specialized instruction in their field, are common in physics; they are taught by researchers chosen for their expertise, and their pedagogy remains almost entirely traditional lecturing. We report a study at a one-week graduate school in neutrino and astroparticle physics attended by 38 students from 8 countries. Six lecturers with limited or no prior experience of teaching with active learning introduced Peer Instruction-style active learning into their lectures after approximately four hours of preparation led by a physics-education specialist. Each of the three main lecture streams was taught traditionally on one day and in the active format on the other. Students were surveyed after each of the first two days and at the end of the week; all six instructors were surveyed as well. In every active lecture, spanning three instructors and three topics, 82-100% of respondents reported that the interactive segments helped them stay engaged and check their understanding, and in every stream the difficulty was rated as better matched on the active day. At the end of the week, 33 of 36 wanted such teaching in future graduate courses, and not one respondent disagreed. All six instructors judged the approach productive and intend to use it again; only two found implementation straightforward. With brief expert-led preparation, lecturers new to active learning implemented it successfully for a heterogeneous audience, and both students and instructors want more. The school was also the first time any of the six instructors implemented structured active learning: a summer school can develop its lecturers as well as its students.

7. When Should Science Act on an Interstellar Anomaly?[2604.20896]
Abstract

An unusual interstellar object may warrant further observation even when there is little evidence that it is artificial. The challenge is deciding which observations are worth pursuing, how much to invest in them, and when to stop. We argue that the Loeb Scale can help guide these decisions, provided its levels are understood as provisional assessments rather than estimates of the probability of extraterrestrial technology. Candidate technosignature status may justify targeted follow-up when new observations could distinguish competing explanations and the expected scientific value warrants the cost. This requires testable predictions and retrodictions, attention to natural explanations not yet considered, and recognition of the risks of false alarms. We place this argument in the broader history and philosophy of scientific anomalies and relate it to existing assessment frameworks in SETI and astrobiology. The cases of 1I/Oumuamua, 2I/Borisov, and 3I/ATLAS illustrate why classifications and research priorities must remain open to revision. Candidate status is a reason to seek better evidence while it can still be obtained, not a conclusion about an object's origin.
