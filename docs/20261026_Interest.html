History & Philosophy of Physics

1. Cartographing Gravity: Operational Maps, the Conatus of Proper Time, and the Potentia of Matter[2610.32569]
Abstract

Classical natural philosophy sought the natural law governing the motion of free bodies long before daring to construct the appropriate mathematical formalism. Following this longstanding methodological perspective, in the present work General Relativity is reconstructed from operational and dynamical principles, rather than taking spacetime geometry as its starting point. The \textit{conatus} of a massive free particle is identified with its natural tendency to follow a worldline of stationary proper time, which, under the appropriate local conditions, corresponds to a maximum of its proper time, whereas gravitation is interpreted as the \textit{potentia} of matter to modify the local rate of proper time. By requiring the exact validity of Galilean free fall, the weak-field static invariant is determined. The gravitational field of moving sources is subsequently obtained by Lorentz-transforming the elementary static solution and causally superposing the retarded contributions of infinitesimal source elements. The resulting linearized field equations coincide with those of General Relativity in the harmonic gauge. Finally, imposing general energy-momentum conservation together with the requirement of second-order field equations yields— within the class of theories satisfying the stated assumptions and by virtue of Lovelock's theorem—the unique covariant completion represented by Einstein's field equations. Within this construction, geometry is not postulated from the outset; instead, it emerges as the final mathematical synthesis of operational dynamics based on proper time, inertia, and free fall.

2. Light pulse propagation through a dielectric slab. I. Retarded integral equations and their local finite-difference solution[2610.29336]
Abstract

We describe the propagation of a nonresonant light pulse normally incident on a thin dielectric slab using an integral-equation approach to electromagnetism. This emerges naturally from the microscopic picture of the material as made of polarizable atoms, confined within the material vacuum volume by their bonding interactions, each responding to the input radiation pulse as well as to the re-radiated pulses of the other atoms to which it is causally connected. The retarded integral equations are shown to be exactly equivalent to a local wave equation coupled to an auxiliary Lorentz-oscillator equation, which we solve numerically with a finite-difference time-domain scheme. For a slab thick enough that the front- and back-surface reflections are temporally separated, the front-surface reflectivity extracted from the simulation agrees with the Fresnel formula to within 1 percent, and the back-surface echo appears at exactly the expected round-trip delay. For a slab thinner than one optical cycle, the multiply-reflected wavelets overlap and interfere, and a full space-time map of the field directly visualizes the build-up of thin-film-like interference in the time domain. The material parameters used here are illustrative rather than fitted to measured data, and the numerical scheme is verified by an explicit grid-convergence study. In a companion paper (Paper II) the retarded integral equations are solved directly, without the local reformulation, and the two independent numerical solutions are shown to agree.
