History & Philosophy of Physics

1. Locality, Probability and the Relevance of History[2610.41944]
Abstract

Relativistic theory and experiments teach us that causal influences are constrained by the speed of light. Deterministic theories incorporate this lesson in $\textit{functional}$ terms: whether an event occurs or not is functionally determined by events that occur in its immediate causal past. In this paper we suggest a natural generalization of this constraint that applies to probabilistic theories: the $\textit{probability}$ that an event occurs is to be functionally determined by the $\textit{probability}$ of the event-occurrences in its immediate causal past. This constraint differs from Bell's criterion of local causality and turns out to imply it only under an additional assumption that requires history to be, in a sense, $\textit{ir}$relevant. Bell-inequality-violating quantum phenomena are thus arguably $\textit{not}$ in tension with relativity, locality, "statistical independence", nor with a supposed "realism". What they instead show is that history is, in a sense, $\textit{relevant}$.

2. DNNs, Dataset Statistics, and Correlation Functions[2511.21715]
Abstract

This paper argues that dataset structure is important in image recognition tasks (among other tasks). Specifically, we focus on the nature and genesis of correlational structure in the actual datasets upon which DNNs are trained. We argue that DNNs are implementing a widespread methodology in condensed matter physics and materials science that focuses on mesoscale correlation structures that live between fundamental atomic/molecular scales and continuum scales. Specifically, we argue that DNNs that are successful in image classification must be discovering high order correlation functions. It is well-known that DNNs successfully generalize in apparent contravention of standard statistical learning theory. We consider the implications of our discussion for this puzzle.

Physics Education

3. "Existential dread": Student and faculty views on the impact of changes in federal science funding to physics research[2610.41699]
Abstract

Sustained investment in physics research is essential not only for advancing science and technology, but also for training the next generation of scientists, including undergraduate students, graduate students, and postdoctoral researchers. Through their research experiences with faculty advisors, these trainees develop critical skills such as experimental design, data analysis, scientific communication, and collaborative problem-solving. Starting in early 2025, the United States administration introduced changes that affected the funding landscape for physics research: grant funding was slashed, grant review panels were delayed or canceled, and some universities froze hiring and research spending. This study aims to better understand the impacts of these changes to academic physics researchers at different career stages. Using survey responses from physics undergraduate students, graduate students, postdoctoral researchers, and associate professors, we find that most academic researchers perceive the impacts to their research as negative. Some of the most commonly reported impacts include loss of existing research funding, concern about obtaining research funding in the future, and job insecurity. We discuss differences in these impacts across career stages and potential implications for the research training pipeline in physics.
