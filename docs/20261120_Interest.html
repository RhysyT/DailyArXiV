1. The BSM Rollercoaster, 1980-2000[2611.25060]
Abstract

This contribution is a personal historical retrospective on beyond-the-Standard-Model (BSM) physics from 1978 to the early 2000s, organized around its emotional rollercoaster. I trace two waves of optimism separated by a multiyear contraction of confidence, both ultimately ending in disappointment when decisive experimental confirmation failed to arrive. The story begins in 1978, when the Standard Model emerged as the front-runner. It then moves from the first wave of excitement over naturalness and Technicolor, rumored proton-decay events, and the construction of the Minimal Supersymmetric Standard Model, to the contraction of confidence in the late 1980s. A dramatic revival came in 1991, when the LEP and SLC precision data pointed toward supersymmetric unification, but it eventually gave way to renewed disappointment over the absence of superpartners and the sharpening of the LEP paradox. I also describe how the realization that Newtonian gravity had been tested only down to distances of order centimeters opened the door to large extra dimensions. Finally, I discuss how Weinberg's 1987 anthropic argument for the cosmological constant, the 1998 discovery of cosmic acceleration, and the emergence of the string landscape changed the intellectual status of naturalness itself and led to Split Supersymmetry. I have tried to describe not only what was proposed, but why these ideas felt compelling at the time, why confidence rose and fell, and how later evidence changed our sense of what could count as an explanation.

2. Colloquium: Isotopes for medicine and fundamental physics[2611.25145]
Abstract

Isotopes, variants of elements distinguished by their neutron number, have important applications for medicine and fundamental science. This review explores the historical challenges and new technologies for enriching and detecting isotopes. We motivate the work by challenges posed by three of history's greatest scientists: James Clerk Maxwell's thought experiment on thermodynamics, Ernest Rutherford's practical dilemma in radiochemistry, and Albert Einstein's questions about the probabilistic nature of quantum mechanics. The journey to meet these challenges has catalyzed the development of innovative techniques for isotope separation. These advancements can mitigate the worldwide crisis in the supply of enriched isotopes. We then explore the expected impact of these new technologies on medicine, which include radioisotopes for cancer therapy and stable isotopes as tracers for metabolism. We explain the importance of developing scalable techniques for isotope detection and how it can help with early detection of certain diseases. Finally, we turn to basic science, outlining proposed new tests of quantum mechanics that utilize radioactive atomic clocks to probe the fundamental nature of determinism and distinguishability of atoms.

3. The two-component picture of soft hadron production: Regge exchange, duality, and resonance cascades[2611.14388]
Abstract

The fragmentation regions of soft hadronic collisions approach energy-independent limits. Benecke, Chou, Yang, and Yen stated this hypothesis of limiting fragmentation in 1969, and Feynman gave it a parton-model formulation the same year. Measurements from the CERN Intersecting Storage Rings (ISR) to the Large Hadron Collider (LHC) show this approach. Fischer and co-workers interpret the NA49 spectra from the CERN Super Proton Synchrotron (SPS) as the sum of a target and a projectile component, each the decay of an excited hadronic system. This review traces the theory behind that two-component picture. It examines the hypothesis that the hadronic tools that predate quantum chromodynamics (QCD) suffice for the soft, non-perturbative domain of QCD. Regge exchange organizes the fragmentation region in triple-Regge form and supplies the diffractive mass law of the excited systems. Finite-energy sum rules and the two-component duality of Harari and Freund equate averaged towers of resonances with that exchange. Duality therefore motivates describing the excited systems in hadronic degrees of freedom. The cascade description has five predecessors, namely the fireballs of Niu, the novas of Jacob and Slansky, the clusters of Hwa, Good-Walker diffraction, and the two-stage dissociation of Hama. A companion paper ( arXiv:2611.11570 ) assembles these pieces into HEART (Hadronic Excitations And Resonance Transitions), an event-level cascade of two-body hadronic transitions, and fits it to the NA49 data. The review closes with the fragmentation-region measurements that would separate a resonance cascade from a string, namely the isospin of forward baryons, local strangeness balance, and forward resonance signals.

Physics Education

4. Particle physics science communication with grass-roots Instagram: a success story[2611.25806]
Abstract

Communicating science to the public is an integral part of the work of a research collaboration, not an afterthought. Institutional and collaboration-level social media accounts are often run by professional communicators rather than by practising scientists, and single-scientist “influencer” accounts are demanding to sustain. We describe {\em CMSatDESY}, a crowd-sourced Instagram account on which the CMS group at DESY tells its own stories, each member in their own voice. In the first half of 2026, the science communication activities through this reached 140k+ unique accounts and received 250k+ views, with an engagement rate of 7\%. We discuss experiences and best practices that can be used by other groups, and describe how the account doubles as a science communication playground for outreach training for a large particle physics group.

6. The Ionospheric Cut-off as a Potential Radio Technosignature[2611.26025]
Abstract

Radio emission generated on a planetary surface must cross the planet's ionosphere before escaping into space: below the local plasma frequency it is reflected back towards the surface. We investigate this process for broadband radio technosignatures (BRaTs) using a Chapman-layer description of photoionised atmospheres. The plasma frequency depends only weakly on stellar XUV irradiation, scaling as $Q_{\infty}^{1/4}$, implying turnover frequencies of 5-40 MHz for most terrestrial habitable-zone planets, potentially rising towards 100 MHz in extreme environments. The basic signature is simple: the continuum of a surface-origin BRaT should be strongly suppressed below a characteristic frequency that can be estimated from the XUV output of the host star, at every orbital phase and for any viewing geometry. Oblique propagation, magnetic fields, and collisional absorption broaden the cut-off into a smooth escape function, $f_{\mathrm{esc}}(\nu,\phi)$, where $\phi$ is the orbital phase. Because the day-night ionisation pattern is anchored to the substellar point rather than to the rotating surface, disc integration adds a second, more discriminating signature: the half-escape frequency $\nu_{1/2}$ varies predictably (by about 40-50 per cent over the orbit, depending on the angular emission pattern of the leakage) and remains locked to the orbital ephemeris. Finite atmospheric response times introduce modest phase lags but preserve the modulation. A low-frequency turnover alone is not unique to technology, but one that varies periodically with the orbital period would be difficult to reproduce through known astrophysical processes. Although observationally challenging, the ionospheric cut-off is a potential technosignature diagnostic and a novel probe of exoplanet ionospheres.
