1. Indefinite causal order with definite Bohmian trajectories for the quantum switch[2611.17906]
Abstract

We analyze the notion of indefinite causal order (ICO) from the standpoint of the deterministic de Broglie–Bohm theory, based on the notion of trajectories in configuration space. More precisely, we study the physics of the quantum switch (QS) interferometer within a Bohmian framework and show how the notion of causality is preserved in this approach. Furthermore, we investigate device-independent certification and causal inequalities applied to an entangled quantum system, and show that in this regime too, the notions of trajectories and of a strict causal order are perfectly preserved.

2. The Good, the Bad, and the Ugly – Living with Priors in Bayesian confirmation[2606.14281]
Abstract

Bayesian confirmation faces a classic problem: where do initial priors come from? In cases with abundant data and repeated updating, different priors tend to converge to the same posterior. However, in frontier research this convergence often fails, and confirmation remains sensitive to priors. We examine how physics practice in the case of gravitational wave research deals with such cases and, normatively, when prior sensitivity should be regarded as epistemically problematic. We offer a practice-based account of the prior problem, drawn from a detailed case study in physics, of a kind so far absent from the philosophical literature on Bayesianism. One upshot is a clearer diagnosis of so-called `analogue confirmation'.

3. Experimental characterization of magnetic multipole fields up to octupole order using smartphone magnetometry[2611.18131]
Abstract

The multipole expansion is one of the organising ideas of electromagnetism: far from a localised source the field is dominated by its lowest non-vanishing moment, and each successive order decays one power of the distance faster. Although the dipole field is routinely measured in introductory laboratories, higher orders are seldom explored experimentally, because the fields are weak, decay very rapidly and require careful positioning. Here we show that with four identical neodymium cube magnets, a sheet of graph paper and the magnetometer of a smartphone, first-year students can build and characterise not one but three distinct magnetic octupoles (linear, planar and `square' arrangements), together with the dipole and the two quadrupoles that serve as reference. A simple notation and a compact set of formulae, based on the spatial Cartesian moments $M_{pq}=\sum_k m_k x_k^p z_k^q$ of the array, predict which field component survives at each measuring position and how it decays. The measured exponents are $n\simeq 3$, $4$ and $5$ for dipole, quadrupole and octupole arrangements, and the measured prefactors show fair quantitative consistency with the independently determined dipole moment of the magnets once nominal geometry and spacer effects are taken into account. We discuss the systematic effects that limit the octupole measurements (higher-order terms at short distance, residual lower-order moments at long distance, the location of the sensor inside the phone) and the ways in which the activity can be extended. The experiment generalises and unifies previous smartphone-based measurements of dipoles, linear quadrupoles and a linear octupole.
