1. Causality, determinism and predictability: Probing nature through classical and quantum eyes[2611.23128]
Abstract

In this article, mainly focussed on students and interested readers, we discuss how the understanding of causality, determinism and predictability is transformed as we move from the classical world view to the quantum description. We first explain how determinism and predictability operate in classical dynamics and outline the notion of causality. A paradigm shift occurs within the framework of quantum mechanics where we have to revisit, and even give up, the notions of determinism, predictability and maybe even strict causality. An engrossing and fascinating debate ensues, after the formulation of quantum mechanics among the giants of physics like Einstein, Bohr, Heisenberg, Pauli, and many others. While some questions have been settled in the last century in favour of quantum world view, there are still many open questions, most important of which is the role of gravity in shaping events within the quantum framework.

2. Learning Effects in Concept Association: A Study with Multidimensional Scaling[2611.21965]
Abstract

This work presents a study designed to investigate learning effects in the association of scientific concepts. In the experiment, high school students worked with advance organizers, as proposed by Ausubel, and with an Arduino board to play songs they had programmed. Numerical Association of Concepts Tests (TANC), administered before and after the instructional intervention, were analyzed using ALSCAL, a multidimensional scaling program available in the SPSS statistical package. The results strongly suggest that TANC is sensitive to changes in students' cognitive structures and that AlSCAL is capable of extracting structural information from the tests.

3. Stress Testing a Multimodal Large Language Model Grading Assistant on Kinematics Graph Reading[2611.22726]
Abstract

Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) can recognize graph features yet award credit without sufficient graphical support. This exploratory study examines selected grading outputs for synthetic responses to four kinematics exercises, generated through visual distortion and progressive transformation toward reference graphs. Different model and prompt configurations were used. Some judgments correctly distinguished instantaneous zero velocity from rest over a finite interval and recognized positive curvature. Yet a severely distorted velocity–time graph received full credit despite disconnected and branching traces and an unclear zero-velocity reference. Other submissions graded under the same configuration lost substantial credit. Numerical cutoffs intended to distinguish meaningful features from drawing irregularities were supplied without a coordinate convention for unnumbered graphs, leaving the basis of exact partial-credit awards unresolved. Stress testing can expose unsupported awards before classroom use. For the configurations examined, we do not recommend accepting scores as final grades without instructor review, including review of full-credit responses.

4. Students' Validation Criteria for AI-Generated Physics Solutions[2606.16830]
Abstract

A physics solution can be clear without being valid. This distinction becomes important when students read AI-generated solutions that appear coherent but still require physical and mathematical checking. We examined how pre-service physics teachers in a calculus-based introductory electromagnetism course evaluated AI-generated solutions to a charged-rod electric-field problem after attempting the problem themselves. Although the AI-generated solutions analyzed were correct, no student accepted a response as valid after a first reading. Students who had solved the problem fully or in part could examine the responses using physical and mathematical criteria, while others withheld acceptance because they could not follow the mathematical development. These results suggest that, in physics instruction, AI-generated solutions should be treated less as answers to be accepted or rejected and more as worked-out responses that students learn to reconstruct, question, and test.

5. FlowMLLab: An open-source framework for reproducible computational-fluid-dynamics and scientific-machine-learning experiments[2609.27975]
Abstract

FlowMLLab is an open-source Python framework for scientific machine learning in thermal-fluid and aerospace engineering, covering incompressible, compressible and rarefied flows. Its 22 weeks comprise 51 notebooks and 35 lectures. Solvers and molecular sampling underpin field prediction, uncertainty quantification and reduced-order modeling. Proper orthogonal decomposition (POD) and deep operator networks (DeepONets), including POD-DeepONet, connect operating conditions to flow fields alongside Fourier neural operators. Physics-informed neural networks infer solutions and constitutive coefficients. Geometry-conditioned models, including the point-cloud DeepONet variant Geom-DeepONet, handle changing domains. Aerospace applications address airfoil shock/vortex identification, rarefied-gas modeling, nozzle flow prediction and supersonic body design. Complementary thermal-fluid examples cover unsteady wakes, hydrofoil vapor clouds and noisy particle observations. Attention mechanisms support sparse-sensor reconstruction, autoregressive prediction and transfer assessment, alongside character-level language modeling. Complete-case evaluation excludes tests from selection; execution notes identify retained research outputs. A worked extension embeds a learned monotone relation for a fixed manufactured Carreau fluid in channel momentum balance. At three unseen in-support pressure gradients, it outperforms profile-interpolation and fitted-Newtonian controls with different information or priors.
