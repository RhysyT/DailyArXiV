1. Distributional Counterfactual Dependence in the Special Sciences[2610.34075]
Abstract

Special-science explanations often concern distributions rather than individual outcomes. Since higher-level states depend on lower-level processes, what would make a higher-level explanation redundant? I argue that redundancy requires replacement without loss, not merely a reconstruction of individual outcomes. Distributional counterfactual dependence (DCD) identifies how changes in higher-level conditions alter an outcome distribution or a relevant feature of it. Against the background of stochastic supervenience, I distinguish this explanatory role from the dependence of higher-level states on lower-level bases. A replacement must be specified independently of the distribution to be explained and preserve the same counterfactual pattern at the grain and across the conditions relevant to the question. Applications to wage distributions, cognitive load, and selection clarify this requirement. A hypothetical comparison shows how a lower-level model can preserve the explanation and why matching selected outcome statistics may not suffice. DCD thus describes one form of non-reductive explanation that depends on counterfactual organization rather than additional higher-level productive powers.

2. Temporal rhythms of uncertainty: Evaluating Mars claims in the Australian colonial press[2610.34178]
Abstract

Favourable oppositions of Mars gave nineteenth-century astronomers recurring opportunities to test claims about the planet, while telegraphic news and newspaper reprinting carried those claims well beyond the professional observatories. Historians have established the importance of these events to the development of mass-media astronomy. Here, I examine a related question in the Australian colonial press: how did the standing of particular claims change as brief reports, local responses and fuller accounts appeared at different times? Drawing on colonial newspaper coverage from 1875 to 1899, I follow episodes surrounding the major Mars oppositions of 1892 and 1894. Reports of observations attributed to "Professor Pickering" in 1892 prompted both rapid circulation and immediate scrutiny. In 1894, a cable about William W. Campbell's spectroscopic work provoked local disagreement before more detailed accounts became available. These sequences show that a Mars opposition concentrated attention without imposing a uniform progression from anticipation to discovery and subsequent caution. Newspapers carried assessments and renewed speculation at the same time. The Australian case extends work on event astronomy by showing how the timing and form of information arriving in a colonial press network affected the public evaluation of astronomical claims.

3. Automated, auditable dating of the Babylonian Astronomical Diaries with language models and astronomical tools[2610.34297]
Abstract

The Babylonian Astronomical Diaries record the sky over nearly six centuries. Many tablets are broken and have lost their date formula. Experts dated many of them one tablet at a time in the standard edition, Astronomical Diaries and Related Texts from Babylonia (ADART). They used the recorded sky and other evidence, such as year formulas and royal names. Our system automates the astronomical part for all tablets with the same rules. A rule-based parser turns the recorded observations into clauses, short statements with fixed fields. A language model checks them against the text and adds observations that the parser missed. Astronomical tools test the clauses in all 701 years from 626 BCE to AD 75. The system ranks the years by how well they agree with the clauses. The system withholds published dates until its results are final, and the output lists the passages and computed positions behind each candidate year. On 120 dated tablets, the years tied at the top of the ranking include the published year in 96% of cases. For 86 of them, this top group is a single year, the published year in 84. In 119 of the 120, the reported candidate set contains it. For 56 undated entries of ADART IV, the system gives ranked candidate years. For each tablet, the results show how far astronomy alone narrows the year. An interactive page at this https URL follows each tablet and links the code, protocol and results.

4. A Personal History in Quantum Foundations: an Oral History Interview[2610.30998]
Abstract

This oral history interview provides Yakir Aharonov's personal and conceptual recollections from the 1950s to the 2000s, focusing on his academic trajectory, some of his major contributions to quantum foundations, and his perspective on the development of the field. The interview traces Aharonov's academic career through the Technion, University of Bristol, Brandeis University, Yeshiva University, University of South Carolina, Tel Aviv University, George Mason University, and Chapman University. The interview also traces the development of Aharonov's work and collaborations on the EPR paradox, time-energy uncertainty relations, quantum measurement theory, quantum frames of reference, the two-state vector formalism, weak values, protective measurements, and several other contributions. It includes recollections of meetings and discussions with David Bohm, Niels Bohr, John Clauser, Paul Dirac, Rudolf Peierls, Aage Petersen, and Abner Shimony. Also mentioned: David Albert, Jeeva Anandan, Peter Bergmann, Gideon Carmi, Avshalom Elitzur, Dave Finkelstein, Vladimir Fock, Itzhak Kalugai, Ed Lerner, Joel Lebowitz, Stanley Mandelstam, Yuval Ne'eman, Sandu Popescu, Maurice Pryce, Daniel Rohrlich, Nathan Rosen, Joseph Safko, Paul Singer, Daniele Struppa, Leonard Susskind, Lev Vaidman, Chien-Shiung Wu.

5. Keep your eye on the tank: A fresh look at an accelerating frame demonstration[2610.33049]
Abstract

A water tank sliding down a frictionless ramp is a classic example of an accelerated reference frame. Along with other accelerated frames, Bartlett analyzed frictionless sliding, and showed that the liquid's surface is parallel to the surface of the ramp, rather than the base. Duzen and coworkers used liquid in an accelerating tank to help students explore motion, noting that the ramp case and riding on a cart accelerated by a hanging mass are equivalent. Munera used the second as a lecture demonstration. In both Refs. 2 and 3, students viewed accelerating tanks from the lab frame, which may be hard to observe at a distance. As the goal of these demonstrations is to understand the effects of the acceleration in the tank frame, tank-frame observations would be beneficial. Following Krulj and Slisko, a camera was mounted to an accelerated object to make in-frame videos of liquid-based equivalence principle demonstrations. Previous work did not quantify the actual acceleration of the tank; use of a Smart Cart simplifies this measurement.
