1. Prestatability in the Macroscopic Limit[2611.09989]
Abstract

We examine whether the variables required for a future macroscopic description of a given physical system can themselves be stated in advance. Representing macroscopic descriptions as coarse-grainings of a fixed microscopic phase space, we distinguish formal prestatability, concerning the existence of an effective complete vocabulary of admissible macroscopic distinctions, from dynamical prestatability, concerning whether the future relevance of those distinctions is decidable. Both obstructions disappear for finite microscopic state spaces. In the infinite limit, exhaustive formal prestatability can fail, while a restricted predictive architecture may remain formally pre-statable. We construct such an architecture and show that, under universal dynamics, the future relevance of a candidate variable is equivalent to the halting problem. Thus a fixed microscopic phase space can support macroscopic descriptions whose future variables are not fully pre-statable.

2. Stanley Deser 19th March 1931 - 21st April 2023[2611.11323]
Abstract

Stanley Deser was Ancell Professor Emeritus at Brandeis University and Visiting Professor at the California Institute of Technology. He was a world-renowned theoretical physicist whose accomplishments include the Arnowitt-Deser-Misner (ADM) formulation of general relativity, supergravity with Zumino, the dynamics of flat space with Jackiw and 't Hooft and many other contributions to the quantum theory of gravity. In the 1980s he was instrumental in securing National Science Foundation approval of the LIGO gravitational wave experiment against much opposition. He was awarded the Einstein Medal in 2015 for important contributions to general relativity, in particular the development of the ADM formalism. Other awards include: The Dannie Heineman Prize 1994; Fellow of the National Academy of Sciences 1994; Honorary Member of the Torino Academy of Sciences 2001; Member of the American Academy of Arts and Sciences 1979, Fellow of the American Institute of Physics.

3. Discovery of the Solution to the "Einstein-Podolsky-Rosen Paradox"[2604.09826]
Abstract

In 1935, Albert Einstein, Boris Podolsky and Nathan Rosen (EPR) published a thought experiment that is entirely correct, has been demonstrated in real experiments, and is now the most famous in quantum physics. Their pioneering work described, for the first time, quantum correlations and can be regarded as a very early glimpse into today's `deep' quantum technologies, by which I mean those that enhance functionality by making use of quantum correlations. However, their work also produces a paradox that Erwin Schrödinger had already recognised as such in 1935 and which has since been cemented by the so-called Bell experiments. Here, I can now pinpoint the cause of the paradox in the EPR line of reasoning. It boils down to a single assumption by EPR, which I prove to be false. I show that this resolves the paradox.

4. Dissipation of Spekkens' Generalised Contextuality under Decoherence: The Classical Limit[2605.09558]
Abstract

When does a decohering quantum system become classical? Generalised noncontextuality gives one criterion, for scenarios rich enough in preparations: a system is classical once its statistics admit an underlying model in which operationally indistinguishable procedures are physically identical. Where it crosses depends not on the state and channel alone but on which preparations, transformations and measurements the experiment offers, and if every measurement is allowed there is no crossing. For any pairwise unbiased bases in any dimension, given preparations holding their eigenstates and surrounding the maximally mixed state, the question reduces to a finite linear program; for a qudit of odd prime dimension $d$ restricted to the stabiliser subtheory, with one depolarised magic state, we solve it in closed form. With preparations and measurements alone the qutrit strange state is classical at zero noise; with the Clifford gates added, each channel represented once, it crosses at 3/5; and with a random gate represented, as Spekkens' treatment asks, by the matching mixture of stochastic maps, at 3/4. A Clifford orbit of assignments supports such a representation only if it has $d^2$ members, leaving, in every odd prime dimension, only Gross's discrete Wigner function: the crossing sits where it stops being negative.

5. The Logic of Emergence[2611.06641]
Abstract

“Emergence” refers to the fact that different mathematical languages and theories seem to be required to describe scientific theories that operate at different spatial and temporal scales. Highly circumscribed, special cases of emergence have been investigated using many different formal tools, including coarse-graining (over space and / or time), renormalization, dimension reduction, broken symmetry, and various formulations of causality. Here we extend the mathematics of model theory to unify and extend these earlier investigations. As a preliminary application of our framework, we investigate canonical examples of emergence, including the emergence of genetics from chemistry, of psychology from neurobiology, and of different forms of collective behavior from the laws governing their constituent parts. By exploiting the power of model theory we also uncover many aspects of emergence that were previously obscured. In particular, we uncover some of the rich hierarchical structure that relates different scientific fields. For example, we show that scientific fields can be related via a collection of multiple partial orders, all defined over the same set. On the other hand, under an alternative relation among scientific fields, one which allows cycles, we show that it is possible that the set of all of scientific fields must collapse into a single scientific field. Going forward, these investigations point to the formal tools that might need to be developed in order to arrive at a deeper understanding of this emergent hierarchy of scientific reality.

6. Aircraft speed and height from Doppler-shifted sound and flight-track data[2611.12033]
Abstract

The changing pitch of a passing aircraft is a familiar example of the Doppler effect. We use audio recordings of 40 aircraft passages to estimate the speed and height by two methods based on the Doppler effect. The endpoint method is an analytical model that uses the limiting frequencies at the start and end of each recording and the slope near the middle of the frequency-time curve. The second method fits the whole curve. Results from both methods are compared with independent flight-tracking data. The endpoint method consistently underestimates speed and height because the recorded frequencies have not approached their limiting values. The full curve fit agrees with the tracking data within about 3% in speed and 12% in height for steady passages, with poorer agreement when speed or height changes. Software developed with AI assistance made it practical to analyze this many passages. The comparison offers undergraduate students an example of testing model assumptions and identifying systematic errors in real measurements.

7. How light interacts with matter, revisited[2611.10171]
Abstract

In 1968 Victor Weisskopf explained in Scientific American how light interacts with matter, with a few formulas and many words. We tell the same story again with the tools that have since become available: the fields are computed and drawn, and their evolution in time is shown in animations. Light is described as a classical electromagnetic field and matter as a collection of charged oscillators; every phenomenon follows from adding up the retarded waves radiated by the oscillators, each with its proper delay and phase. From this single picture we obtain the blue of the sky and the white of the clouds, transparency and the refractive index, the Ewald-Oseen extinction theorem, reflection and the colours of thin films, why metals reflect and what gives metals and semiconductors their colours, polarizers, Brewster's angle and birefringence, the working of mirrors, lenses, microscopes and the eye, the propagation of a few-cycle pulse through a slab, Cherenkov radiation, amplification and second-harmonic generation. Feynman's arrows are made quantitative with the sheet method, and coupled-dipole simulations show the incident wave being cancelled and replaced inside the medium. The connection with quantum theory is discussed where the classical picture stops: the photon, spontaneous emission and the forbidden lines of the aurora, vision and photosynthesis, and the sum over paths. The text is addressed to undergraduate students and their teachers, and to any reader curious about the physics behind everyday optical phenomena; derivations are collected in separate panels, and the animations and the Python codes used for the figures and simulations are provided as ancillary files.

8. Cosmic Corpses: Dead stars as Factories of the most Extreme Objects in the Universe[2611.09988]
Abstract

The apparent serenity of the night sky conceals a long history of stellar evolution, in which gravity and the properties of matter determine the remarkable fates of stars. In this pedagogical account, we discuss white dwarfs, neutron stars, and black holes, explaining how quantum mechanics, nuclear interactions, and General Relativity govern their formation and distinctive properties. We also highlight the possibility of primordial black holes, which may have formed from rare large density fluctuations in the early Universe, and briefly discuss their potential role as constituents of dark matter.

9. The Physics of Interstellar Travel[2611.10651]
Abstract

How can we send an uncrewed spacecraft to the nearest stars within a human lifetime? What propulsion methods are available? Will it survive impacts in the interstellar medium? What science will it do at its destination? How can it send back data over enormous distances? In this short semi-popular article, I examine various aspects of the physics of interstellar travel, its challenges and possible solutions. I focus on nuclear rockets, laser sails, dust impacts, and communication. These, as well as other issues such as navigation and onboard power, are explored in more detail in my book, The Physics of Interstellar Travel (2026).

10. Work needed to lift a mass in Schwarzschild spacetime[2611.11879]
Abstract

We determine the work needed to lift a mass from $r_2$ to $r_1$ in Schwarzschild spacetime in three ways. First we use a winch and a light rope. Then we use a photon collision. Finally we let the mass decay into photons and reconstruct it at the higher radius. All three methods give the same local work at $r_1$, $W_1=mc^2[1-N(r_2)/N(r_1)]$, where $N(r)=\sqrt{1-r_s/r}$. Thus, independently of $r_1$, the work tends to $mc^2$ as $r_2$ approaches the horizon. The rope example also gives a useful general-relativistic version of a simple machine: a rope in equilibrium need not have equal local forces at its two ends. The redshift factor plays the role of the lever arm. For a real rope the energy conditions prevent the tension from decreasing with height, so this lever advantage is confined to the idealization. The examples are intended as teaching material for undergraduate relativity courses.

11. The Optics of Shadow Bands[2606.28366]
Abstract

Shadow bands are transient, rippling patterns of light and dark that may appear in moments before and after totality in a solar eclipse. Despite centuries of reports, their physical origin has remained unresolved. This article develops a geometric-optical solution in which the Sun's extended structure produces a celestial analog of Young's double-slit experiment, generating an interference and fringes on the ground. The analysis combines solar limb geometry and a wave-based formulation that yields quantitative predictions for fringe width and spacing. Some other properties of shadow bands are also analyzed.
