1. A Gravitomagnetic Bootstrap: from Newtonian gravity to general relativity via localizing rotational and conformal symmetries[2611.20210]
Abstract

Newtonian gravity, in the light of the Equivalence Principle, is best understood as a localization of Galilean inertial structure — or at least, of the part of Galilean inertial structure that defines rectilinear acceleration. I consider what kind of theory can be constructed if we also localize rotational inertia and spatial and temporal scales. The resultant theory, given weak assumptions, is unique up to a constant with the dimensions of velocity and an overall sign. For an appropriate choice of these, it coincides with the first post-Newtonian approximation to general relativity, so that this construction gives a method to bootstrap from Newtonian gravity to general relativity without explicitly presupposing special relativity.

3. Why Cassini's Oval Could Not Replace Kepler's Ellipse?[2611.21336]
Abstract

In the later seventeenth century, Kepler's ellipse was not yet accepted. Ward claimed that Boulliau's conic hypothesis amounted to a Ptolemaic equant at the ellipse's empty focus, and built his own model on it. Around 1675–1680, Giovanni Domenico Cassini proposed another curve, the “oval”, a genus-1 quartic with no closed-form Kepler equation; yet its eccentric anomaly shares exactly the leading coefficient of Kepler's, and its empty focus is, for the Sun, 38 times closer to a true equant than the ellipse's. Dynamically, Binet's formula shows that the force producing the oval under the area law departs from the inverse square by an index $\mathcal{D}(e)\sim3/(4e^2)$, depending on the oval's parameter $e$: no single exponent serves the whole family. This absence of universality, not of an area law, distinguishes it from the ellipse, and arguably explains why Cassini, the Académie and Leibniz kept the question open after the \emph{Principia} (1687).

4. Working with writing experts to enhance a physics curriculum[2611.18903]
Abstract

A common observation of physics faculty and professionals hiring physics graduates is that undergraduate students have deficient physics-specific writing skills, in particular difficulties with writing genres such as abstracts, reports, articles, proposals, and posters. Students are not often exposed to such authentic writing experiences as part of their curriculum, and faculty may not feel qualified to teach writing. Scholarship focused on writing transfer, defined as the ability to apply the writing skills and knowledge learned in one context in a new writing context, suggests that students need support with transfer-focused writing skills such as genre and audience awareness and that these are skills in which students can be trained. For physics students, physics faculty are best placed to do this as successful participants in their disciplines. We present an interdisciplinary effort, by faculty of physics and writing studies, of developing a writing in physics curriculum using a scaffolded approach including three courses in the second through fourth year of an undergraduate physics program. This approach uses threshold concepts from writing studies, such as genre, audience, and purpose. Students are given the methodological tools to approach writing, and the instruction includes structured peer feedback that is connected to the instructor's feedback and grading. This effort led to improvements in the written and oral communication skills of students in a broad range of genres and for various audiences, evidenced, for instance, by an increase in successful fellowship applications. The course materials developed for this effort are publicly available to the community.

5. Microstate Response Model: A Unified IRT Framework from Latent Nominal States to a Theory of Tests[2611.21164]
Abstract

Several features outside of the conventional assumptions in traditional item response theory (IRT) models have been revealed to be related, yet no unifying framework explains the features simultaneously. They can be unified by treating each cause as a distinct reason, and then regarding every reason as a choice in a latent multiple-choice item. Here, we propose the Microstate Response Model (MiRM), which decomposes each response category (macrostate) into multiple latent reasons (microstates) using Boltzmann weights. The consequences of this simple framework are substantial; for example, it reads the lower and upper asymptotes of item characteristic curves (ICCs) as additional states, and ICC asymmetry and item complexity as multiple states. The model unifies many classical models from the dichotomous logistic family to mixture models. Moreover, the algebra of the model provides the intuition that items and tests are the same object. Two consequences naturally come out: partial credits and test scores are unified, and a new test-level monotonicity condition is proposed. We examine item characteristics including nonmonotonicity on PISA 2015, and show that MiRM provides a deeper understanding of items while achieving the strongest overall performance across fit, parsimony, and prediction.

6. Immersive virtual reality in learning physics: a systematic review of opportunities and challenges[2611.21228]
Abstract

Immersive virtual reality (IVR) can heighten presence and enable active, embodied interaction in realistic 3D environments, which has been associated with potential benefits in physics education. This article presents a comprehensive review of IVR implementation in physics education at both school and university levels. The analysis included 34 studies indexed in Scopus and ERIC, published between 1 January 2018 and 1 June 2025. Methodologically, this review followed a preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses approach. IVR implementations for physics learning were assessed, topic-aligned activity types were summarized, and associated opportunities and challenges were documented. Findings indicate that IVR can support physics learning by providing complementary visualizations, optimizing cognitive load, enabling haptic learning, saving time, and fostering collaborative inquiry. Conversely, the most frequent potential IVR challenges include discomfort, unreliable interaction, orchestration issues, relatively high costs, and significant extraneous cognitive load.

7. Is Gravity a Real Force or Merely a Geometrical Effect?[2611.19644]
Abstract

Whether gravitation should be regarded as a fundamental force or as a manifestation of spacetime geometry remains an interesting conceptual question in physics. In Newtonian mechanics, gravitation is described as a force acting between massive objects, whereas General Relativity describes gravitational phenomena in terms of spacetime curvature, with freely falling bodies following geodesics. With this fundamental difference, it is legitimate to ask whether gravity is a real force or not, and it is very common to receive from experts a somewhat condescending answer such as "Drop a rock on your foot and you will feel that the force is real". This paper does not attempt to determine whether gravity is fundamentally a force or a geometrical phenomenon. Rather, it questions whether commonly invoked everyday experiences provide valid grounds for deciding between these interpretations.
