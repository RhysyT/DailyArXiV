1. A Personal History in Quantum Foundations: an Oral History Interview[2610.30998]
Abstract

This oral history interview provides Yakir Aharonov's personal and conceptual recollections from the 1950s to the 2000s, focusing on his academic trajectory, some of his major contributions to quantum foundations, and his perspective on the development of the field. The interview traces Aharonov's academic career through the Technion, University of Bristol, Brandeis University, Yeshiva University, University of South Carolina, Tel Aviv University, George Mason University, and Chapman University. The interview also traces the development of Aharonov's work and collaborations on the EPR paradox, time-energy uncertainty relations, quantum measurement theory, quantum frames of reference, the two-state vector formalism, weak values, protective measurements, and several other contributions. It includes recollections of meetings and discussions with David Bohm, Niels Bohr, John Clauser, Paul Dirac, Rudolf Peierls, Aage Petersen, and Abner Shimony. Also mentioned: David Albert, Jeeva Anandan, Peter Bergmann, Gideon Carmi, Avshalom Elitzur, Dave Finkelstein, Vladimir Fock, Itzhak Kalugai, Ed Lerner, Joel Lebowitz, Stanley Mandelstam, Yuval Ne'eman, Sandu Popescu, Maurice Pryce, Daniel Rohrlich, Nathan Rosen, Joseph Safko, Paul Singer, Daniele Struppa, Leonard Susskind, Lev Vaidman, Chien-Shiung Wu.

2. From Venus to the Parking Lot: A Flexible Stellar Interferometry Experiment[2610.30669]
Abstract

We describe a simple extension of our earlier Venus interferometry demonstration that can be performed indoors or outdoors, independent of planetary visibility or atmospheric seeing. A small flashlight is placed 15-50 meters away from a telescope and covered with a circular aperture mask (hole diameter 2-6 mm). A small telescope fitted with an opaque mask bearing two 2 mm holes, spaced between 5 and 20 mm apart, is then used to observe interference fringes from this artificial extended source. By varying the hole separation and observing the fringes students can estimate the angular diameter of the source and verify their result directly by simple geometry, a check not possible with astronomical targets. The experiment requires no telescope tracking, no dark skies, and no special equipment beyond materials described in the original paper. It serves equally well as a pre-lab to build intuition before observing Venus, a standalone indoor daytime or outdoor nighttime activity, or a quantitative test of the interferometric method itself.

3. "I don't have a good metric for what other people think:" Unpacking the double empathy problem in neurodivergent physics problem-solving[2609.21809]
Abstract

Learning is a social act and requires communication between interlocutors. Learning also is a process that takes place in a socially constructed neuro-normative system. These neuro-normative systems can marginalize neurodivergent (i.e., autistic, dyslexic, epileptic) students, who oftentimes have non-normative socio-cognitive differences. In this paper, we investigate 3 neurodivergent physics students' experiences solving physics problems and in navigating physics culture. We use a Double-Empathy Problem lens to uncover how communication, particularly communication breakdowns, can manifest for neurodivergent students in physics. We find that the 3 neurodivergent students, experience communication breakdowns in their interactions with peers and instructors, and in interpreting problem statements. Furthermore, we find that participants experience fear of being misinterpreted or feeling as though they are viewed as stupid on account of communication breakdowns. We advocate for the need for a more neuro-inclusive physics culture and suggest actions that can be done by instructors to create more inclusive spaces in physics.
