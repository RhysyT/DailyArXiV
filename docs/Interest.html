1. On the relationship between the POVM framework and the no-signaling hypothesis[2610.22823]
Abstract

The quantum foundations literature contains dozens of "no-signaling" arguments that rely on the Born rule or, more generally, the positive operator-valued measure (POVM) framework to show that the resources of non-relativistic quantum mechanics (NRQM) cannot be used to send superluminal signals. What these arguments typically establish, however, is not a prohibition on superluminal signaling per se, but the seemingly stronger claim that signaling at any speed between mutually isolated systems is forbidden. This paper gives a precise formulation of this claim, the no-signaling hypothesis, and investigates the following converse question: under what additional assumptions does it imply that all experimental outcome probabilities are described by POVMs? Surprisingly few are needed: given the standard NRQM representation of composite experiments and two textbook claims about marginal probabilities, the no-signaling hypothesis is equivalent to the universality of the POVM framework. More broadly, by formalizing the relevant concepts and deriving the logical relationships between them, the paper shows that the no-signaling hypothesis can be used to derive most, though not all, of the probability-generating fragment of NRQM. The resulting map of implications clarifies which common postulates of NRQM are logically interrelated and which can be modified independently.

2. On the foundations of anthropic reasoning: Implications for life across the universe[2610.22896]
Abstract

With only one confirmed example, namely the Earth, the abundance of life across the universe remains deeply uncertain, and local evidence about Earth's particular circumstances can significantly shape our knowledge of life elsewhere. Such extrapolation is difficult not only because of this sample size of one, but also due to observer selection effects discussed under the Anthropic Principle. In this paper, we describe and apply a framework called Full Indexical Conditioning (FIC) for performing Bayesian inference in this situation. Inspired by, but distinct from, Full Non-Indexical Conditioning, FIC conditions on all evidence available to the reasoner while considering that this evidence could have been otherwise. We address the reference class problem associated with such reasoning, demonstrating that substantive conclusions are unaffected by the choice of reference class provided it is sufficiently large. We apply FIC to several problems in astrobiology, including the Doomsday argument, the early abiogenesis time, and our location around a G-type rather than M-type star, clarifying the foundations of these arguments. We conclude that the early abiogenesis time does not rule out pessimistic possibilities for life's abundance, and that while life around M stars may be uncommon, its temporal distribution could extend far into the future. Ultimately, FIC offers a clear conceptual framework, grounded in straightforward probability theory, for tackling problems involving anthropic selection effects.

3. Designing an AI Agent That Does Not Tutor: Design Rules for Socratic Probing in Introductory Physics[2610.22949]
Abstract

Instructors need to know whether a student understands a concept, not only whether the student can produce a correct answer, and the two have come apart: a student can paste a physics question into a chatbot and submit a fluent, correct answer without understanding it. We built a conversational AI agent for introductory mechanics that only probes. It is designed as an instrument that a correct answer alone should not pass. Its key measure is cave detection: identifying a correct first idea that the student abandons when a single variable of the scenario is changed. A single right-or-wrong item cannot see this. The agent asks a student to commit to a prediction about an everyday situation, explain it without physics vocabulary, and defend that explanation against a changed case built from the student's own words. It does not teach, hint, confirm, or correct, and it reports nothing until the end. Such an instrument works only if its replies give nothing away. LLM agents tend to leak evaluation: praise, word choice, reply length, and pacing reveal whether an answer was right, and a student who knows stops reasoning. We report design rules that suppress this leakage, derived from eight versions of the agent and more than 40 scripted simulation runs. The obvious fixes often made leakage worse: listing forbidden praise phrases increased praise, and a single worked example in the prompt spread into unrelated conversations. Each changed case had to alter exactly one variable; otherwise a changed answer could not be traced to a single idea. The evidence is simulation evidence only: no real students have used the agent, and the author wrote both the personas and the scoring rules. This paper reports design findings, not learning outcomes.

4. "I don't have a good metric for what other people think:" Unpacking the double empathy problem in neurodivergent physics problem-solving[2609.21809]
Abstract

Learning is a social act and requires communication between interlocutors. Learning also is a process that takes place in a socially constructed neuro-normative system. These neuro-normative systems can marginalize neurodivergent (i.e., autistic, dyslexic, epileptic) students, who oftentimes have non-normative socio-cognitive differences. In this paper, we investigate 3 neurodivergent physics students' experiences solving physics problems and in navigating physics culture. We use a Double-Empathy Problem lens to uncover how communication, particularly communication breakdowns, can manifest for neurodivergent students in physics. We find that the 3 neurodivergent students, experience communication breakdowns in their interactions with peers and instructors, and in interpreting problem statements. Furthermore, we find that participants experience fear of being misinterpreted or feeling as though they are viewed as stupid on account of communication breakdowns. We advocate for the need for a more neuro-inclusive physics culture and suggest actions that can be done by instructors to create more inclusive spaces in physics.

5. Six objects reported at 480 mph in a declassified military infrared video show brightness flicker at bird wingbeat rates[2610.08692]
Abstract

In September 2026 the U.S. Department of War released an infrared video recorded from a military aircraft over the Middle East, with a mission report describing six small spherical objects moving at an estimated 480 miles per hour. We measure the brightness of each object in every frame. All six brighten and dim steadily at 7.1-7.9 times per second, each at its own rate and out of step with the others. Tests with planted constant-brightness objects show that neither compression nor the sensor produces this flicker. The same analysis, applied to the Galileo Project's ground-based infrared cameras near Las Vegas, finds flying animals beating steadily at 3.4-10.0 times per second, within the range of bird wingbeats. A nearly stationary object at 60-80% of the aircraft's height would produce the reported ground-projected speed of 480 miles per hour. The six objects are best explained as a small flock of birds flying nearer to the aircraft than the crew assumed. The same analysis also finds 19 objects with similar beats, at 2.3-11.0 times per second, in nine other released military videos. Releasing the redacted sensor and platform data would provide information that could refute the bird hypothesis

6. Missed Detections and False Alarms in a CNN Technosignature Detector on Breakthrough Listen Data[2610.22050]
Abstract

Radio technosignature searches rely on machine learning detectors to shrink the candidate pool before a human looks at it. They are validated on held-out data from the training distribution, which excludes the cases where they are most likely to fail: a technosignature has unknown morphology, and terrestrial interference is unbounded in variety. I trained a standard competition detector once, froze it, and probed it with 15,000 injections across ten morphologies and five amplitudes, placed into real Green Bank backgrounds. Two directions were tested: genuine signals with morphologies other than a straight narrowband line, and interference resembling the training data. Recall did not follow the predicted ordering, and it fell as signals brightened, even for a needle of the kind the detector was trained on (from 0.590 to 0.090). One interference morphology reached a false positive rate of 0.627, comparable to the detector's own baseline recall of 0.5525 on genuine signals. Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) showed attention along the injected path in one missed family by a pre-specified test, and visually in a second. A pre-specified, time-averaged ON-OFF filter removed false alarms and genuine detections at the same rate. The detector reads only the ON panels. Since the only evidence distinguishing a genuine candidate from terrestrial interference lies in the discarded OFF panels, it cannot tell if a signal originates from the target star; it can only tell if it resembles the training data. This explains the false alarm directly; the missed signals reflect what the detector learned to recognise, which reading the OFF panels would not by itself change.
