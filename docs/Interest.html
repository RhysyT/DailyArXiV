1. Active Learning with Generative AI in Introductory Physics: A Multi-Institutional Study of the P-A.I.R. Framework[2611.04148]
Abstract

Generative AI tools have become part of how students study physics, yet unstructured use may reduce students' opportunities to work through problems independently. The Physics AI-Replication (P-A.I.R.) framework structures AI use as a four-step cycle: students identify a challenging problem, obtain an AI-generated explanation and solution, request similar practice problems, and solve them independently before reviewing the AI's solutions. We evaluated P-A.I.R. in 11 algebra- and calculus-based introductory physics sections at four U.S. institutions using a common end-of-semester survey. Most students reported improved conceptual understanding (90.9\%) and found the replicated practice problems helpful (97.7\%), and these two ratings were strongly associated ($r_s = 0.58$). Perceptions of both components, as well as post-engagement confidence ($\mu = 7.18$ on a 1-10 scale), were consistent across institutions. Post-engagement confidence was associated with perceived improvement in conceptual understanding ($r_s = 0.37$) and the helpfulness of replicated practice ($r_s = 0.35$), but not with how often students used AI ($r_s = 0.02$) or with self-reported GPA ($r_s = 0.13$). Thematic analysis of open-ended responses showed that students valued AI primarily for conceptual clarification and structured problem solving (63.0\%), while their main concerns were AI accuracy (34.2\%) and the need for explanations that guide rather than simply provide answers (29.7\%). These findings suggest that how students engage with AI matters more than how often they use it, and that frameworks sequencing AI assistance with independent problem solving offer a practical, transferable approach for physics instruction.

2. Triboelectric nanogenerators across length scales: linking materials, multiscale modelling and applications[2611.04008]
Abstract

Triboelectric nanogenerators (TENGs) convert low-frequency mechanical energy into electricity and enable self-powered sensing. Their performance depends on interactions among material properties, surface morphology, contact mechanics, charge generation and external circuits. This review examines these interactions across length scales to assess how material and interface descriptions support predictive device design. Polymeric, inorganic, composite and natural materials are discussed in relation to charge generation and retention, mechanical compliance, durability and application requirements. Modelling approaches span molecular dynamics and first-principles calculations, rough-surface contact mechanics, device-scale electrostatics and equivalent circuits. Their capabilities are assessed through the inputs they require, the quantities they predict and the experimental evidence used for validation. Particular attention is given to prescribed surface charge density, idealised geometries and nominal contact-area assumptions, which constrain predictions under realistic loading conditions. Applications are organised by macroscale, milliscale and microscale device dimensions to examine requirements for materials, architecture and integration. The review identifies evolving real contact area and contact-dependent charge generation and decay as critical links between interfacial behaviour and electrical output. A staged validation strategy is proposed for contact mechanics, electrostatics and circuit response, with independent testing beyond calibration conditions. These priorities guide the development of multiscale models and reliable TENG systems.

3. Roadmap to Quantum Aesthetics[2602.08363]
Abstract

Quantum mechanics occupies a central position in contemporary science while remaining largely inaccessible to direct sensory experience. This paper proposes a roadmap to quantum aesthetics that examines how quantum concepts become aesthetic phenomena through artistic mediation rather than direct representation. Two complementary and orthogonal approaches are articulated. The first, a pioneering top-down approach, employs text-prompt-based generative AI to probe quantum aesthetics as a collective cultural construct embedded in large-scale training data. By systematically modulating the linguistic weight of the term "quantum," generative models are used as experimental environments to reveal how quantum imaginaries circulate within contemporary visual culture. The second, a bottom-up approach, derives aesthetic form directly from quantum-mechanical structures through the visualization of quantum-generated data, exemplified here by hydrogen atomic orbitals calculated from the Schrödinger equation. These approaches are framed not as competing methods but as intersecting paths within a navigable field of artistic research. They position quantum aesthetics as an emergent field of artistic research shaped by cultural imagination, computational mediation, and physical law, opening new directions for artistic practice and pedagogy at the intersection of art, data, artificial intelligence and quantum science.
