History & Philosophy of Physics

1. Turning Old Anomalies Into New Evidence: Evidential Reconfiguration and Dark Matter in 1974[2512.12926]
Abstract

The 1974 papers by Ostriker et al. (1974) and Einasto et al. (1974) are regarded as pivotal in establishing the evidential case for dark matter, although both relied on already existing observational data. By what means did they transform known observational anomalies into evidential support for dark matter? I argue that this transformation arose through evidential reconfiguration: extended galactic halos supplied a physical connection between galaxy rotation curves and cluster mass discrepancies, expanding the context of assessment so that results previously investigated locally could constrain one another. The significance of this connection was assessed against differing cosmological commitments and unresolved local dynamical assumptions, which help explain both the emphases of the two papers and their reception. To explicate its contribution to confirmation, I draw on Myrvold's Bayesian account of unification and use a toy model in which taking one result into account changes the assessment of a shared halo parameter, thereby altering expectations about the other. Comparison with an uncoupled model makes the resulting support explicit, while variations of the model show why a shared parameter alone is insufficient: the contribution depends on the connecting assumptions and what competing models predict. The novelty relevant to the old-evidence problem thus lies in what familiar observations became jointly capable of supporting. This reconstruction assesses that support once the connection is specified, while leaving a full account of historical belief change open.

Physics Education

2. Predictive Modeling for High Impact Active Learning Classrooms[2603.14335]
Abstract

Over the past several decades, a large body of research has shown that undergraduate science students learn more and more equitably in active learning classrooms; however, the term "active learning" lacks definition and little research has examined which types and combinations of associated strategies are most effective. This study uses data from 69 courses, 3 scientific disciplines, and 24 institutions to create a predictive model that maps classroom time spent on different activities to student conceptual learning. We find that a model using four classroom variables – time spent on lecture, group worksheets, group clicker questions, and student questions – can make predictions that account for 49\% of the variance in effect sizes, as measured by $R^2$. We identify one type of class that is predicted to have exceptional student learning gains (effect sizes greater than 2): those that spend 10-20% of class time on group worksheets, 20-40% of class time on group clicker questions, and 10\% or more of class time on student questions. We also find that classes that do not utilize group worksheets have learning outcomes comparable to fully lecture classes. These results provide testable recommendations for future controlled studies to investigate effective active learning implementation in undergraduate physics courses.

3. The Optics of Shadow Bands[2606.28366]
Abstract

Shadow bands are transient, rippling patterns of light and dark that may appear in moments before and after totality in a solar eclipse. Despite centuries of reports, their physical origin has remained unresolved. This article develops a geometric-optical solution in which the Sun's extended structure produces a celestial analog of Young's double-slit experiment, generating an interference and fringes on the ground. The analysis combines solar limb geometry and a wave-based formulation that yields quantitative predictions for fringe width and spacing. Some other properties of shadow bands are also analyzed.
