History & Philosophy of Physics

1. The Geometry of the "Gauge Argument"[2610.26049]
Abstract

We show how the "gauge argument", widely criticized by philosophers as ad hoc and unconvincing, may be reconstructed rigorously, and, we believe, convincingly, as an argument concerning the geometry of vector bundles. In this form, the gauge argument has two parts, the first of which is strongly analogous to the Weyl-Stein argument for Galilean spacetime as the appropriate setting for Newtonian gravitation; and the second of which invokes a classic result in the Yang-Mills theory literature, due to Utiyama [1956, "Invariant Theoretical Interpretation of Interaction", Phys. Rev. 101, 1597].

2. Rethinking Symmetry Principles and Partial Observables With Reference Frames[2410.12892]
Abstract

Dynamically uncoupled fields are a modelling device used in the literature on reference frames, classical and quantum. We argue that the device is not innocuous. First, uncoupled fields violate Earman's symmetry principle SP1—that every dynamical symmetry is a spacetime symmetry—and the violation, unlike the familiar Newtonian one, cannot be repaired by excising surplus spacetime structure: it arises from a deficit of dynamical structure, not an excess of spatiotemporal structure. This qualifies implicit assumptions of Earman's SP1, while leaving its diagnostic role intact. Second, uncoupled fields expose a lacuna in the partial/complete observables programme: only dynamically coupled fields can serve as bona-fide partial observables, so partial observability is derivative on complete observability. We then reinterpret the standard formalism for partial observables: qua measurable, a partial observable is a relational quantity expressed with respect to an uncoupled frame, and it remains gauge-variant. This reinterpretation vindicates Rovelli's claim that partial observables are individually measurable, against Thiemann and Adlam, and separates two properties easily conflated in the generally covariant formulations of theories: invariance under diffeomorphisms and invariance along the gauge flow.

3. Becoming, Duration, and the Evolving Block Universe[2607.03415]
Abstract

The two-times problem asks how the directed, oriented time of biological and conscious systems relates to the time of fundamental physics. The Evolving Block Universe, in which spacetime grows through an irreversible quantum dynamics along physically preferred worldlines, accounts for the direction of physical time and for its emergence across scales up to the time kept by the brain. It leaves open the relation between this physical time and the experience of passage. We argue that Bergson's durée, the continuous qualitative experience of lived time, names this residue and marks the first-person level at which the physical hierarchy reaches its limit. On this reading the two accounts divide the problem between them: the Evolving Block Universe describes its structural, third-person half, and durée its experiential, first-person half. The remaining gap between the two halves is explanatory. It is an instance of the familiar gap between the physical description of a system and the character of its states as they are lived, and it is compatible with physicalism.

4. Connectivism as an Innovative Didactic Experience in Preservice Physics Teacher Education[2610.25620]
Abstract

This chapter examines the use of connectivism as a pedagogical approach in preservice physics teacher education through a classroom experience conducted in a Contemporary Physics course at the Universidad Pedagógica y Tecnológica de Colombia (UPTC). The study involved 21 preservice teachers organized into four collaborative groups. Using a qualitative approach, students selected and discussed digital learning resources, interacted through WhatsApp, and prepared collaborative reports connecting contemporary physics concepts with scientific and technological applications. The analysis considered conceptual understanding, argumentation, questioning, critical thinking, relationships among concepts, and the use of digital resources. Students' reports and feedback indicated favorable perceptions of the collaborative activities and provided examples of conceptual connections, evidence-based explanations, and reflection on future teaching practices. The chapter discusses the possibilities of connectivist learning networks for physics teacher education, drawing on the findings of this particular classroom experience.

5. The chair challenge: can mechanics really tell men from women? A centre-of-mass analysis of a viral party trick[2610.25187]
Abstract

In the "chair challenge" a person stands several foot lengths from a wall, bends forward until the head touches the wall, lifts a chair and tries to stand up. Online videos suggest that women can do this and men cannot, supposedly because women have a lower centre of mass or men have larger feet. We model the body as a planar chain of rigid segments with standard biomechanical parameters and search all postures allowed by the rules for the one that places the combined centre of mass of body and chair furthest back. As a rule of thumb, the largest toe-wall distance is roughly the trunk-plus-head length minus the distance from hip to centre of mass. For a slow, static rise this limit is 2.36 foot lengths for the male and 2.49 foot lengths for the female reference model, just short of the customary 2.5 foot lengths. The limit is set mainly by trunk-plus-head length relative to foot length, not by the height of the centre of mass; in centimetres the male model actually has more room. With the body proportions of 6068 participants in the 2012 US Army anthropometric survey, the sex difference shrinks from 0.13 to 0.03-0.05 foot lengths, half to one European shoe size. Individually, only 13% of men and 22% of women would pass statically, and predicting sex from the outcome is right in 54-68% of cases (for equal numbers of men and women), against 50% for a coin and 83% for a tape measure. Shoes, age and momentum matter more than the residual sex difference. The problem suits first-year undergraduate mechanics, combining static equilibrium of an articulated body, scaling and units, an inverted-pendulum estimate and a simple classification problem. The chair challenge is a fine demonstration of static equilibrium and a lousy sex predictor.

6. Pulsars as natural launchers of interstellar probes[2610.26454]
Abstract

In this study, we investigate the feasibility of accelerating small probes up to relativistic energies via magnetocentrifugal acceleration by pulsars. Specifically, we consider a broad range of pulsars and examine probes with radii ranging from $10$ to $1000$ nm. We show that the probes may attain Lorentz factors of up to approximately $25$. Furthermore, we investigate the detectability of such probe swarms and demonstrate that, if a highly advanced civilization deploys probes with a total mass of approximately one billion tonnes for acceleration, their collective synchrotron emission could be detectable by the FAST radio telescope at a distance of 1 kpc. The study also discusses the potential resemblance between the emission from such probe swarms and fast radio bursts (FRBs), and outlines directions for future research.
