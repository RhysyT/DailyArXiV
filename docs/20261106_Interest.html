1. Between Heisenberg and Feynman: Ettore Majorana's Reflections on the Meaning of Quantum Theory[2611.07143]
Abstract

Ettore Majorana is undoubtedly one of the most important figures in 20th-century Italian science. His contributions to nuclear physics and Quantum Mechanics (QM) included innovative ideas, and the search for Majorana-type neutrinos remains an ongoing experimental research question. However, very little is known about his perspective on the significance of the quantum state, the philosophical implications of the then-new mechanics, and the relationship between QM and other sciences. This chapter aims to address this gap. In more detail, here I present the standard interpretation of Majorana's view on the quantum state, according to which the Italian physicist anticipated the physical content of Feynman's path-integral formulation of quantum theory. In what follows, I critically examine this position and propose an alternative understanding of Majorana's beliefs on the nature of the quantum state and the wave function. Finally, I briefly discuss his reflections concerning the major conceptual revolutions introduced by QM, notably, in his view, the impossibility of extending the objectivity and determinism of classical mechanics to the quantum realm, and their consequences for the relation between physics and the social sciences, disciplines that Majorana deeply studied before his disappearance.

2. A Theory of Partial Truth for Scientific Statements[2611.07179]
Abstract

Scientific representations often combine accurate and inaccurate components, which a single bivalent classification does not distinguish. This paper develops a restricted formal framework for estimating partial factual truth. For fixed content and valuation criteria, the target is adequacy to the facts, independent of the available evidence; its numerical assessment through evidence remains fallible. Quantitative claims are evaluated by normalized discrepancies between asserted and empirically supported values. Admissible finite conjunctions are assessed over a fixed basis of distinct elementary claims using arithmetic or relevance-weighted means. The resulting valuations preserve the contributions from accurate components and are independent of grouping when component counts or total weights are retained. Zero-relevance additions leave the contextual valuation unchanged, but compensation remains possible among components with positive relevance. Worked examples examine admissibility, changes of granularity, measurement indeterminacy, and sensitivity of comparisons to discrepancy scales and relevance weights. An astrophysical application connects the assessment to published observations. The framework applies to selected testable consequences of models and theories, without providing a numerical calculus for arbitrary formulas or a unique method for choosing scales and weights. It permits conditional comparisons of factual adequacy while distinguishing them from confidence, empirical coverage, and convergence toward a complete truth.

3. On Algebraic Background-Independence[2610.22042]
Abstract

It has recently been claimed that algebraic methods can be used to give a background-independent description of semiclassical gravity. This paper aims to provide an entry point into recent algebraic approaches to quantum gravity and to analyse and critically assess the conception of background-independence here involved. We discuss an algebraic framework for formulating an algebra of observables along the worldline of an observer in a cosmological setting. By 'gravitationally dressing' the observables to the observer's worldline, the reference to the spacetime background can be set aside at the level of the abstract *-algebra. Thus this *-algebra is taken to be independent of any particular spacetime realisation, with different states on the algebra corresponding to different backgrounds. We compare this algebraic framework to three influential notions of background-independence in the literature and argue that none of these fully captures the sense in which the *-algebra is taken to be background-independent. Building on this, we extract a novel notion of algebraic background-independence tied to the representation-independence of the *-algebra. This algebraic conception motivates two further concepts of background-independence tailored to semiclassical theories: semiclassical background-independence and perturbative background-independence. We suggest that these provide useful tools for evaluating the scope and limitations of semiclassical approaches to quantum gravity.

4. Balancing broomsticks to a t![2611.06942]
Abstract

A classic question from rotational mechanics asks, "Is it easier to balance a broomstick heavy side up or heavy side down?" Measuring the time a student can balance an asymmetrically weighted meter stick in two orientations is a straightforward exercise that can be used to introduce or review the t' framework, which guides lab students in comparisons of results and iterative improvement of their measurements. As a two-year college physics program, many of our students attend from other institutions, so while t' is taught in the introductory mechanics labs, there is no guarantee that a student in a following lab course will be familiar with the material. To address this, the t' concepts are reviewed in the first session of subsequent lab courses. The broomstick experiment is a quick and fun implementation.

5. Getting the picture - Subtractive color mixing with an inkjet printer[2611.06943]
Abstract

When teaching colors, physics teachers ask their students to ignore what is taught in art classes and embrace a primary color system of red, green, and blue. This scheme is additive and all colors can be generated by combining light of these three colors with varying intensities. There have been a number of demonstrations and tools to help students understand additive color mixing, using LEDs, color monitors, or similar technologies. However, the subtractive primaries of cyan, magenta, and yellow have received far less attention, if students have previous knowledge it is from printer ink cartridges. This gap may exist because as pigments it can be harder to vary their intensities in real time. Fortunately, our colleagues in the art department are well-versed in subtractive color mixing, and have taught this topic for many years through the lens of color printing. We steal a page from their book and describe how to create a color image using cyan, magenta, yellow, and black layers (CMYK). Black is used as an additional layer due to mixing issues concerning the imperfections of the CMY pigments. With imaging software, an inkjet printer, and some transparencies, physics instructors are just a few keystrokes away from being able to demonstrate subtractive color mixing.
